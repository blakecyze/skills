# The tones

Named voice profiles for `mimesis-tone`. This is L1, read on every run alongside
[tells.md](tells.md) and [craft.md](craft.md).

A tone is which *human* voice to write in. De-slopping is the separate job of not
sounding like a *machine*. The two compose: every tone here runs the same linter
and the same craft rules, so no tone ever reintroduces an em dash, a kill-list
word or a negative parallelism. Tone shapes the voice; the linter keeps it clean.

**The master key, true for all five tones.** The authentic version of every voice
is more *specific* than its AI imitation. The machine reaches for abstraction
because abstraction is safe and statistically common; it sands off the unusual,
concrete, checkable detail. So whatever the tone, the strongest single move is to
replace a vague claim with a specific one: a number, a name, a date, a real
condition. If a sentence could have been written for any reader about any subject,
it is slop regardless of how the tone is styled.

Tone is also not register. Register ([register.md](register.md)) is how
buttoned-up the grammar is, professional or casual, and it layers onto any of
the five voices via `--register`: a warm-professional support reply, a
practitioner-casual blog post. The profiles below say nothing about register
because they do not need to; they compose with either.

Each profile below gives the use, the move, the slop twin to avoid, the craft, and
a tone-specific kill-list on top of the universal one in tells.md.

---

## plain [seeded]

**Use for:** documentation, instructions, explanations, anywhere comprehension is
the only goal.

**The move:** say it so it cannot be misread.

**Avoid (the slop twin):** the flat explainer voice. "Let's break this down",
"let's unpack this", "think of it as", jargon used to sound expert, long chains of
subordinate clauses, abstraction where a concrete noun would do.

**Craft:**
- Common, short words. Anglo-Saxon over Latinate: use, not utilise; start, not
  commence; about, not regarding.
- Subject-verb-object. One idea per sentence. Short paragraphs.
- Concrete nouns and active verbs. Name the thing.
- Cut every word that does not change the meaning. Aim for a low reading grade
  without writing down to the reader.

**Tone kill-list:** utilise, in order to (use "to"), prior to (use "before"), in
the event that (use "if"), "let's dive in", "let's unpack", "let's break this
down", "think of it as".

## blunt [seeded]

**Use for:** opinions, decisions, direct feedback, hot takes made honestly, any
place where softening wastes the reader's time.

**The move:** say the hard thing first, in the fewest words, and back it.

**Avoid (the slop twin):** two opposite failures. One is hedging: "it's worth
noting", "arguably", "that said", "to be fair", reflexive qualifiers that drain the
edge. The other is fake-edgy hot-take cosplay: "Here's an uncomfortable truth",
"unpopular opinion:", manufactured contrarianism where the bold claim is barely
different from the consensus. Real bluntness has a point; the cosplay just has
attitude.

**Craft:**
- Lead with the claim. Cut "I think", "maybe", "perhaps", "in my opinion".
- Take the actual unpopular position and own it, or do not call it blunt.
- Short declaratives. No apology, no cushioning preamble, no false balance.
- Earn the edge with one specific reason or piece of evidence. Bluntness without
  substance is just rudeness, which reads as insecurity.
- Calibrated, not cruel. Sharp about the work, not the person.

**Tone kill-list:** "it's worth noting", "arguably", "that said", "to be fair",
"with respect", "Here's an uncomfortable truth", "unpopular opinion", "hot take",
"let me be honest".

## persuasive [researched]

**Use for:** landing pages, value propositions, hero and CTA copy, case studies,
pricing, cold and warm outreach.

**The move:** specificity carries its own proof. Concrete detail outsells every
superlative.

**Avoid (the slop twin):** the AI marketing register, the single most concentrated
source of slop. In 2026, only 7% of consumers trust a brand more for visible AI
copy and 31% trust it less, so this register actively costs conversions.
- Buzzwords: supercharge, leverage, empower, streamline, unlock, world-class,
  enterprise-grade, best-in-class, cutting-edge, game-changing, seamless,
  transformative, holistic, scalable-and-sustainable.
- Manufactured contrast: "Not just fast. Extraordinary." "It's not software, it's
  your competitive advantage."
- The hero-headline shape: action verb plus vague goal plus superlative
  ("Supercharge your team's productivity"), with no number, product or mechanism.
- Universal tricolon, feature-card bullet bloat, fake urgency ("Don't miss out"),
  hollow superlatives, "hope this finds you well".

**Craft:**
- Hopkins specificity. "33% more light" beats "brighter". Replace every adjective
  claim with a measurement where one exists.
- Voice of customer. Mine real reviews and interviews; use the buyer's own words,
  unpolished, rather than paraphrasing them.
- Harry Dry's three tests for every line: can I visualise it, can I prove it true
  or false, could nobody else write it. Slop fails the third universally.
- Match Schwartz's awareness stage. Do not pitch a "most aware" CTA to a "problem
  aware" reader.
- Ogilvy: the headline does most of the work, write to one person, facts create
  conviction, lead with a benefit. Sugarman: every sentence earns the next.
- Read at grade 6 to 8. Pages under 200 words convert better than long ones. Put
  proof (named testimonials with specific outcomes) next to the friction point,
  not in a separate section.

**Register notes:**
- Hero: a specific problem or mechanism, not a floating transformation promise. A
  CTA that says what happens next ("See your first report in 20 minutes").
- Case study: named person, role, company size, a concrete before-state, the
  mechanism, a specific outcome with timeline, a rough-edged real quote.
- About: lead with why you built it as a genuine frustration; one honest
  imperfection is the strongest authenticity signal.
- Outreach: one specific trigger, one observation not a pitch, under 80 words, one
  low-friction ask, no "hope this finds you well".

**Tone kill-list:** supercharge, revolutionise, disrupt, world-class,
enterprise-grade, best-in-class, game-changing, groundbreaking, holistic,
unparalleled, unprecedented, "in today's fast-paced world", "at the forefront of",
"redefining what's possible", "driving meaningful impact", "hope this finds you
well", "I'd love to connect", "explore potential synergies".

## practitioner [researched]

**Use for:** case studies, portfolio writeups, technical posts, bio and about
copy, talk abstracts. Any writing where you are the one who did the work.

**The move:** credibility is shown, not declared. Ethos comes from the content, in
Aristotle's sense, not from a credential stated before it.

**Avoid (the slop twin):** authority-cosplay. Confident in tone, empty of
substance.
- The word-hoard: landscape, ever-evolving, leverage, harness, delve, foster,
  pivotal, crucial, testament, cornerstone, paradigm, intricate interplay.
- The dutiful five-beat essay (intro, three points, conclusion), rigid
  "Challenges / Future Prospects / Key Takeaways" headers, compulsive summaries.
- Claim without evidence: "research shows", "studies suggest", "many experts
  argue", with nothing named.
- Credential-prefacing ("As a seasoned practitioner..."), false humility, uniform
  over-hedging, manufactured contrarianism, servile positivity where everything is
  notable and significant.

**Craft:**
- Specificity is the primary move. Concrete numbers, named tools, exact versions,
  particular conditions, real dates. The test: can the sentence be falsified. If
  not, it is content-free.
- Show the work and the dead ends. Name what you tried that failed and why. This
  is structurally impossible to fake, because it needs real experience of a
  specific failure.
- Name real tradeoffs. State the conditions under which your recommendation breaks
  down. "This works under 10GB; above that the join spills to disk."
- Earned opinion grounded in your own observation, not general authority. "We
  doubled deploy frequency after requiring a one-paragraph risk note per PR" beats
  "documentation is crucial".
- Calibrated confidence. Hedge selectively where real uncertainty exists, boost
  ("reliably", "definitely") where things are in fact reliable. AI hedges
  uniformly and rarely boosts; do the opposite where warranted.
- Open on substance, not context-setting. Vary sentence rhythm. Drop the summary
  paragraph unless the piece is genuinely long.

**Register notes:**
- Case study: open with the number, not the background. Include at least one wrong
  turn. Exact metrics with conditions ("p95 latency 340ms to 90ms at 500
  concurrent users").
- Portfolio: lead with the actual decision and why ("chose X over Y because Z"),
  show the reasoning and alternatives, name your specific contribution.
- Technical post: name the exact environment and scale; teach something a
  five-minute search would not; say where the advice stops applying.
- Abstract: state the actual finding, not "results will be discussed".
- Bio: active verbs for things built, not adjectives for qualities possessed.
  "Builds data infrastructure at fintech scale", not "passionate, results-driven".

**Metrics, presented credibly:** always give the baseline ("from X to Y"), the
conditions, and the limit of the measurement. "47%" beats "nearly half" beats
"significant uplift". Acknowledge confounders.

**Tone kill-list:** ever-evolving landscape, "in today's fast-paced world",
leverage, harness, pave the way, shed light on, pivotal, testament, cornerstone,
paradigm, "seasoned", "results-driven", "thought leader", "research shows" (without
a citation), "some experts argue" (without names), "in conclusion".

## warm [researched]

**Use for:** onboarding and empty states, welcome copy, transactional and
lifecycle email, error and support messages, community posts, personal outreach.

**The move:** warmth is specific and restrained. Treat the reader as a capable
peer, not a problem to manage. Honest, clear, kind, in that order.

**Avoid (the slop twin):** the imitation of warmth, which often scores higher on
surface empathy yet reads hollow, and which a 2026 study found generates more
negative emotion than a human message once detected.
- Reflexive intensifiers doing the work specificity should: deeply, truly,
  genuinely, sincerely, incredibly, "we deeply care".
- Fake-intimacy openers: "Here's the thing", "Let me be real with you", "Here's an
  uncomfortable truth".
- The template empathy sequence (validate, paraphrase, pivot, resolve): "We
  completely understand how frustrating this must be. Your experience matters to
  us. We're looking into it." Three sentences saying nothing.
- "It's understandable that", "we appreciate your patience", "we value your
  feedback", passive over-apology ("mistakes were made"), hollow enthusiasm and
  emoji padding, generic flattery, grandiose stakes for a minor occasion,
  pedagogical "let's dive in".

**Craft:**
- Specificity and generosity. Give the reader what they actually need. A real
  detail proves you noticed; a generic sentiment proves only that someone filled
  the space.
- Treat the reader as a peer. Use "you" more than "we". Use contractions. Do not
  over-explain or pre-empt objections they have not raised.
- Earned humour only. Mailchimp's rule: if unsure, keep a straight face. Forced
  humour is worse than none.
- Restraint over gush. Cut the reassurance sentence the reader did not ask for.
  "Your files are backed up automatically" needs no "we know how important your
  data is to you."
- Calibrate apology to severity, in active voice. "We got this wrong. Here's what
  happened and what we've fixed." No apology at all for a trivial slip.
- Every message ends with a clear next step.

**The kindness-versus-saccharine test:** does the sentence give the reader
something, or perform the giving. "Your password needs at least 8 characters, try
again" is warmer than "Oops! We know this can be frustrating!" because it is
direct and useful.

**Register notes:**
- Onboarding: start with what the reader can do, not how you feel they are here.
  Empty states say what goes here and how to put it there.
- Error and support: name what happened specifically, give a likely cause, give
  one next step. Do not validate assumed frustration before explaining anything.
- Lifecycle email: acknowledge the real status, and offer the exit genuinely
  ("here's how to delete your account"). Offering the door builds more trust than
  blocking it.
- Community: no "Great question!", no identical acknowledge-explain-resolve-sign-off
  on every reply. Match the room's register.
- Outreach: one specific thing that proves you engaged with their work, one
  genuine question, short, no "hope this finds you well". The peer test: would you
  send this to a colleague you respect.

**Tone kill-list:** deeply, truly, genuinely, "Here's the thing", "it's
understandable that", "we appreciate your patience", "we value your feedback",
"your experience matters to us", "we're so excited", "we can't wait", "hope this
finds you well", "don't hesitate to reach out", "Great question", "mistakes were
made".

## Choosing and combining

Pick the tone from the job, not the mood. A case study is practitioner. A landing
page is persuasive. An error message is warm. A design rationale or a piece of
direct feedback is blunt. Documentation is plain.

Tones can layer lightly (a warm-but-plain onboarding note, a blunt-but-practitioner
post-mortem), but do not stack all five. If a piece needs two, name the dominant
one and let the other adjust the edges. When in doubt, the master key holds across
all of them: get more specific.
