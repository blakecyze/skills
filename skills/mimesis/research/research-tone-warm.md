# The Warm Voice: Authentic vs AI-Generated (2026)

*A practitioner reference for onboarding copy, welcome messages, email, support, error messages, community posts, and personal outreach*

***

## Executive Summary

Warmth is one of the sharpest diagnostic edges in writing today. The machine imitation of warmth is everywhere — in product copy, transactional email, support replies, and community posts — and experienced readers are developing an intuitive resistance to it. The pattern is paradoxical: AI-generated "warm" copy often scores higher on surface empathy ratings than human writing yet reads, over time, as hollow, because it is structurally formulaic and informationally empty. Genuine warmth, by contrast, is specific, restrained, and earns its register by treating the reader as a capable equal rather than a problem to be managed.[^1][^2][^3]

This document catalogues the tells of the imitation, articulates the craft of the genuine, draws the line between warmth and saccharine, gives register guidance by format, and ends with a quick-reference kill-list and positive checklist.

***

## 1. The Slop Twin: How AI Fakes Warmth

### 1.1 Lexical Tells

**Evidence strength: high — documented by practitioners, corpus linguistics, and community analysis.**

The most reliable lexical flags fall into three families.

**Reflexive intensifiers and magic adverbs.** AI reaches for adverbs — *deeply*, *fundamentally*, *remarkably*, *quietly* — to inflate mundane statements into significance. These appear in warmth copy as *"we deeply care about your experience"* or *"we're truly here for you"*: the adverb does the emotional work that specificity should do. Related words: *genuinely*, *sincerely*, *incredibly*, *absolutely*, *certainly*. A corpus study of AI-generated text (Shalevska, 2024) found AI uses hedges like *"may"* at very high frequency while avoiding boosters that carry rhetorical conviction, producing prose that feels both over-qualified and over-polished simultaneously.[^4][^5][^6]

**The "delve" family.** *Delve* became the canonical AI tell because it appeared in a statistically anomalous proportion of AI text. The broader family includes *leverage* (as a verb), *robust*, *streamline*, *harness*, *utilise*, *empower*, and *foster*. These cluster in onboarding and support copy disguised as warmth: *"We're here to empower your journey."*[^6]

**Ornate nouns where plain words would do.** *Tapestry*, *landscape*, *ecosystem*, *paradigm*, *framework*, *synergy*, and *journey* replace concrete descriptions. Warmth copy is especially prone to *journey* (the user's onboarding journey) and *community* used as an empty collective noun.[^6]

**The "Serves As" dodge.** Replacing the simple copula *is* with *serves as*, *stands as*, *marks*, or *represents*. AI's repetition penalties push it towards fancier constructions. In welcome copy this appears as: *"This moment marks the beginning of your experience with us."*[^6]

**Hollow enthusiasm markers.** Exclamation marks in quantities that no composed adult uses, emoji padding that substitutes visual affect for earned tone, and sign-offs like *"Exciting things ahead!"* or *"We can't wait!"* without any content that justifies the excitement.[^7][^8]

### 1.2 Structural Tells

**Evidence strength: high — practitioner consensus, tropes.fyi documentation.**

**The fake-intimacy opener.** *"Here's the thing."* *"Here's the kicker."* *"Here's what most people miss."* These manufactured-suspense transitions promise a revelation and deliver an observation that needed no build-up. They are the structural equivalent of the person at a dinner party who leans in and says *"Can I be honest with you?"* before saying nothing candid. Related: *"Let me be real with you"*, *"Here's an uncomfortable truth"*, *"Here's what nobody tells you"*.[^9]

**Negative parallelism as fake depth.** *"It's not X, it's Y."* The single most-cited AI writing tell across practitioners. Alex Hormozi noted this pattern explicitly in a March 2026 Instagram post reaching millions. Blake Stockton's *Don't Write Like AI* series documents how AI uses this contrastive structure to manufacture profundity: *"We're not just building a product, we're creating an experience."* One instance may work; ten in a piece is an insult to the reader. Includes the causal variant: *"not because X, but because Y."*[^10][^11][^12][^13]

**Dutiful reassurance.** The reflex to tell the reader their feelings are valid before addressing the actual problem. Microsoft Research (2026) studied 3,265 AI-generated empathy responses and found they are *"highly formulaic at a discourse functional level"*, matching a template — validate, paraphrase, pivot, resolve — in 83–90% of cases. Human responses, by contrast, are far more varied. The template manifests in support copy as: *"We completely understand how frustrating this must be. Your experience matters to us. We're looking into this."* Three sentences that say nothing specific.[^3][^14]

**The "it's understandable that" hedge.** A reflexive HR-courtesy formula that precedes almost any complaint acknowledgement in AI-generated support. Adjacent phrases: *"We appreciate your patience"*, *"We value your feedback"*, *"We're committed to providing the best experience"*. These signal that nobody thought carefully about the specific situation — they triggered a template.[^15]

**Over-apology.** Corporate apologies written by or through AI use vague remorse, generic accountability, and promises to *"learn from this experience"* without acknowledging what specifically happened. The passive construction is the giveaway: *"Mistakes were made"*, *"This situation occurred"*, *"Issues were encountered"* — passive voice that apologises for the existence of a problem while no one takes ownership.[^16][^15]

**Short punchy fragments as manufactured emphasis.** RLHF training has pushed models toward "writing for readability" at the lowest common denominator: one thought per sentence, no mental state-keeping required. No real person writes first drafts in this style: *"He published this. Openly. In a book. As a priest."* In warmth copy it appears as emotional staccato — *"We heard you. We listened. We changed."*[^17]

**The tricolon compulsion.** AI has a deep addiction to the rule of three. In warmth copy: *"We're here to help. We're here to listen. We're here for you."* One good tricolon per document is fine. They become an tic quickly.[^11]

**Listicle in a trench coat.** When asked not to use bullet points, AI writes prose that structures content as numbered paragraphs: *"The first thing to know… The second consideration… The third aspect…"*[^17]

### 1.3 Tonal Tells

**Evidence strength: high — corpus-based research and practitioner critique.**

**Smoothed neutrality / customer-support sheen.** Research by Cornell (2025) found that AI writing assistants homogenise style, pulling diverse voices toward a standardised, Western-centric tone. A separate corpus-based study found that AI *"exhibits a deficit in rhetorical conviction and reader engagement characterised by a detached stance"* and *"underuse of boosters and rhetorical questions"*, producing a voice that cannot build a genuine writer-reader relationship. This manifests as the tonal equivalent of beige: safe, inoffensive, palatable, and forgettable. One journalist described corporate AI copy as *"emotional beige paint — polished enough for a press release, dull enough not to offend anyone"*.[^18][^19][^20][^16]

**Warmth with no specifics.** Genuine warmth requires something actual — a real detail, a true observation, a specific acknowledgement. AI warmth gives you the sentiment without the content: *"We love having you as part of our community"* without knowing anything about you. The tell is that the compliment, reassurance, or expression of care could apply to any reader in any situation.[^15]

**False vulnerability.** Simulated self-awareness or honesty that reads as performative. *"And yes, I'm openly in love with the platform model"* — the model pretends to break the fourth wall while remaining entirely risk-free. Real vulnerability is specific and uncomfortable; AI vulnerability is polished. This is the mechanism behind phrases like *"I'll be honest with you"*, *"I want to be transparent"*, or *"This might be controversial, but…"* followed by something uncontroversial.[^9]

**Grandiose stakes inflation.** Everything is the most important thing ever. A blog post about API pricing becomes a meditation on the future of civilisation. In warmth copy this manifests as *"joining us is the beginning of something transformative"* for signing up to a to-do list app.[^9]

**The pedagogical default.** *"Let's break this down."* *"Let's unpack this."* *"Let's dive in."* *"Think of it as…"* The model defaults to teacher mode and assumes the reader needs hand-holding. In welcome copy this becomes: *"Think of this email as your roadmap to getting started."*[^9]

**Vague attributions.** *"Experts say…"*, *"Studies show…"*, *"Many users find…"*. In support copy this appears as *"Our team has found that most users in this situation…"* without naming anything specific.[^9]

## 2. The Authentic Craft: What Makes Human Warmth Read as Real

### 2.1 The Foundation: Specificity and Generosity

**Evidence strength: high — practitioner consensus across multiple traditions.**

The single most reliable marker separating genuine warmth from its imitation is specificity. Specific writing proves that the writer actually noticed something. Generic writing proves only that someone occupied the space where writing should go. Nicole Fenton and Kate Kiefer Lee, in *Nicely Said* (2014), identify the core commitment as being *"honest, clear, and kind"* — in that order. Kindness that is not grounded in honesty and clarity becomes sentiment; sentiment without substance becomes saccharine.[^21]

Generosity toward the reader is distinct from flattering the reader. It means:
- Giving the reader information they actually need, not information that makes the writer feel good.
- Assuming the reader is capable: Ogilvy's *"The customer is not a moron. She is your wife."* — respect their intelligence.[^22]
- Not wasting the reader's time with preamble, reassurance they didn't ask for, or enthusiasm they haven't earned.

The plain-language tradition, associated in the UK with Sarah Winters (née Richards) and GOV.UK, and in the US with the *plain English* movement, converges on the same principle: clarity is the most profound form of respect for a reader. Warmth does not require sentiment; it requires precision delivered without condescension.[^23][^24]

### 2.2 Treating the Reader as a Peer

**Evidence strength: high — Mailchimp style guide, Nicely Said, practitioner consensus.**

The Mailchimp voice and tone guide articulates this as being *"genuine"* — speaking in *"a familiar, warm, and accessible way"* — and *"plainspoken"*, stripping away *"hyperbolic language, upsells, and over-promises"*. Their test: Mailchimp *"values clarity over entertainment"* and would rather be clear than funny. This resolves the most common warmth error: using humour or friendliness to avoid committing to a direct statement.[^25]

Treating the reader as a peer means:
- Not over-explaining. If the reader can figure it out, let them.
- Using *you* more than *we*. The writer's job is to serve the reader, not to narrate the company's feelings.[^26][^27]
- Using contractions (*we're*, *you'll*, *it's*) — the absence of contractions is one of the clearest signals of a formal, distancing register.[^27]
- Not pre-empting objections they haven't raised. *"You might be wondering…"* is presumptuous unless the thing really is widely wondered.

The "write like you talk" tradition — Ogilvy's *"Write the way you talk. Naturally."*, The Writer's version *"write more like you speak, not literally as you speak"* — captures the register without authorising stream-of-consciousness. The test is reading copy aloud: if it doesn't flow in your natural speaking voice, revise.[^28][^29][^30]

### 2.3 Earned Humour

**Evidence strength: medium-high — Mailchimp, practitioner sources, newsletter analysis.**

Mailchimp's voice guide describes their humour as *"dry… straight-faced, subtle, and a touch eccentric"* — *"weird but not inappropriate, smart but not snobbish"* — with the explicit warning: *"don't go out of your way to make a joke — forced humour can be worse than none at all. If you're unsure, keep a straight face"*. This is the key distinction between earned and performed humour.[^25]

Earned humour works because:
- It is specific to the actual situation, not generic.
- It emerges from the material rather than being applied to it.
- It does not interrupt the reader's task; it completes it more memorably.
- It does not require the reader to recognise it as humour to be useful.

Innocent Drinks' newsletters consistently demonstrate this: cheeky subject lines paired with content that earns the tone. The UK brand voice tradition (Innocent, Monzo, Oatly) achieves warmth through wit and economy — one well-placed observation rather than sustained performance.[^31]

The opposite of earned humour is *hollow enthusiasm*: exclamation marks scattered without cause, words like *exciting*, *amazing*, *awesome*, and *incredible* used to prop up content that has not earned them. Readers do not feel the enthusiasm; they feel manipulated into performing it.

### 2.4 Restraint Over Gush

**Evidence strength: high — practitioner consensus, corpus research.**

Over-statement is the most common warmth failure. The AI empathy template (validate, paraphrase, pivot, resolve) produces responses that are *more liked on first encounter than human responses*, but this surface-level palatability comes at the cost of trust over time — because the formula is recognisable and its recognition produces suspicion. The same dynamic operates in welcome copy, lifecycle email, and error messages: the more a piece of copy tries to express care, the more it has to prove that care is real.[^3][^15]

Restraint operates through:
- **Omission.** Cut the reassurance sentence the reader didn't need. Cut the preamble. Cut the sign-off enthusiasm. What remains is more felt.
- **Understatement.** *"This was our fault. Here's what happened and what we've fixed."* versus three paragraphs of empathy formulas followed by a passive-voice explanation.
- **Trusting the content.** If the information is genuinely useful, it does not need emotional framing. *"Your files are backed up automatically."* requires no *"We know how important your data is to you."*

The GOV.UK school is instructive here: government digital content is warm not because it is effusive but because it is written in plain English, addresses the reader directly, and tells them exactly what they need to know without waste. Warmth through efficiency.[^23]

### 2.5 Kindness vs Saccharine: The Diagnostic

**Evidence strength: medium — practitioner consensus, no single definitive source.**

Kindness in writing is *useful* and *specific*. Saccharine writing is *sentimental* and *generic*. The test: does this sentence give the reader something, or does it perform the giving?

| Quality | Kind | Saccharine |
|---------|------|------------|
| Error message | "Your password needs at least 8 characters — try again." | "Oops! We're sorry, something went wrong. We know this can be frustrating!" |
| Welcome | "You're in. Here's what to do first." | "Welcome to the family! We're SO excited to have you here! 🎉" |
| Apology | "We got this wrong. Here's what happened and what we've changed." | "We sincerely apologise for any inconvenience this may have caused. We value your experience." |
| Thank-you | "That feedback was useful — we've already filed it with the team." | "Thank you so much for sharing your thoughts with us! Your input is invaluable to us!" |

The right column is not hostile; it is hollow. It performs care without committing to any specific act of care. The left column is warmer precisely because it is direct.

## 3. The Boundary: Where Warmth Tips Over

### 3.1 The Diagnostic Tests

**Evidence strength: medium — practitioner consensus.**

Warmth tips into saccharine when:
1. The emotional register is higher than the occasion warrants.
2. The sentiment is generic (could apply to any reader, any situation).
3. The writing tells the reader what to feel rather than giving them something to feel.
4. Enthusiasm is performed through formatting (exclamation marks, emoji) rather than earned through content.
5. The apology or empathy comes before — and substitutes for — any actual response to the situation.
6. The writer's comfort is prioritised over the reader's information need.

Warmth tips into fake intimacy when:
- The writer claims a relationship that hasn't been established (*"As a member of our family…"*).
- The writer performs vulnerability that carries no real risk (*"I'll be honest with you…"* followed by something safe).
- The writer uses first-name informality without the right to it.
- The voice adopts the register of a close friend to a stranger.

### 3.2 Worked Examples

**Welcome message**

❌ *AI/saccharine version*
> Welcome to the Bloom community! 🎉 We are SO thrilled to have you here! You're now part of an amazing family of people just like you, and we can't wait to see what you'll create. Here's the thing: this is just the beginning of something truly special. We're here for you every step of the way!

Problems: hollow enthusiasm, the word *family* used as empty collective noun, *"Here's the thing"* as fake-intimacy opener, grandiose stakes inflation (*"something truly special"*), performative over-excitement.

✅ *Human version*
> You're in. Your workspace is ready — nothing's been set up yet, so the first thing worth doing is [importing your first project →]. If anything's unclear, just reply to this email; it goes to a real inbox.

What it does: gets to the point immediately, gives the reader a specific next action, makes the support offer concrete (real inbox, not a chatbot hedge).

***

**Error message**

❌ *AI/saccharine version*
> Oops! We're so sorry about that! 😕 It's completely understandable to feel frustrated — this kind of thing can be really annoying! We're working hard to make sure this never happens again. Your experience truly matters to us. Please try again later.

Problems: over-apologises without saying what went wrong, generic empathy formula, gives no actionable next step, hollow reassurance.

✅ *Human version*
> Payment didn't go through. This is usually a card limit or a temporary issue on our end — try again in a minute, or use a different card. [Contact support →] if it keeps happening.

What it does: states the problem plainly, gives a likely cause, gives two specific next steps, offers escalation without demanding the reader use it.

***

**Thank-you (after a support interaction)**

❌ *AI/saccharine version*
> Thank you so much for reaching out to us today! Your satisfaction is our top priority, and we truly value the opportunity to assist you. We hope this has been a helpful experience. Please don't hesitate to contact us again if you need anything at all. We're always here for you!

Problems: generic, performative, tells the reader the company's feelings rather than acknowledging the reader's experience, hollow sign-off.

✅ *Human version*
> Glad that's sorted. Your account is set up correctly now — you should be able to log in straight away. If anything's off, hit reply and you'll reach me directly.

What it does: confirms the resolution specifically, tells the reader what to do next, makes the follow-up path concrete and personal.

***

**Personal outreach (cold or warm)**

❌ *AI/saccharine version*
> Hi [Name], I hope this message finds you well! I came across your work and was truly blown away — you're doing amazing things in this space! I'd love to connect and explore some potential synergies between what you're doing and what we're building. Would you be open to a quick chat? I think there's some really exciting alignment here!

Problems: *"hope this finds you well"* is a dead phrase, generic flattery (what work? which things?), *"synergies"* as a lexical tell, hollow enthusiasm, asks for a meeting without saying what it would accomplish.[^16]

✅ *Human version*
> Hi Sam — I read your piece on variable pricing models last week and had a question about the churn data you mentioned. We're seeing something similar with annual subscribers but the pattern is inverted. Worth a short call if you're curious? Happy to share our numbers in return.

What it does: names the specific thing, asks one specific question, makes the value exchange explicit, gives the other person a reason to say yes that is about them.

## 4. 2026 Register Guidance by Format

### 4.1 Onboarding and Empty States

**Evidence strength: medium — practitioner sources, UX writing consensus.**

The onboarding moment is high-stakes because it establishes tone for everything that follows. The common failure is treating onboarding as a performance of friendliness rather than a transfer of useful information. AI-generated onboarding copy tends to front-load enthusiasm and delay the first useful instruction.[^32]

**What reads as authentic:**
- Start with what the reader can do, not how you feel about them being here.
- Empty states should tell the reader what goes here and how to put something there — not celebrate the emptiness (*"Your canvas awaits!"*) or over-apologise for it.
- Use the person's name once, at most. Repeated name-use reads as a sales script.
- Welcome email subject lines that skip *"Welcome to X"* for *"You're in — here's what's next"* or *"Your [product] is ready"* read as more confident and peer-level.[^33]

**What reads as AI:**
- *"We're excited to have you on board!"* in the first sentence.
- A list of the company's values before a single piece of actionable guidance.
- Empty-state copy that is just an emoji and a vague encouragement.
- Progress indicators that over-celebrate small completions: *"Amazing! You've added your first item! 🎉"*

### 4.2 Transactional and Lifecycle Email

**Evidence strength: medium — email marketing practitioner sources, Mailchimp guidance.**

Transactional email (receipts, confirmations, password resets) should be brief, accurate, and direct. The warmth register here is functional: plain language, second-person address, contractions. The failure mode is adding emotional framing to a purely informational message: *"We know your time is precious — here is your order confirmation!"*[^28]

Lifecycle email (re-engagement, win-back, renewal reminders) is where AI warmth is most detectable, because it is where companies deploy empathy templates at scale. The pattern — *"We've missed you! We know life gets busy…"* — is so widely recognised that it has become counter-productive.[^15]

**What reads as authentic in lifecycle email:**
- Acknowledge the specific status honestly: *"You haven't logged in for three months — your account is still here if you want it."*
- Offer the exit genuinely: *"If this isn't useful any more, here's how to delete your account."* This is the paradox of genuine warmth — offering the door builds more trust than blocking it.
- If there's a change or offer, say what it is specifically, not that *"something exciting is coming."*

**What reads as AI:**
- *"We value your continued support."* (hollow courtesy)
- *"We've been thinking about you."* (fake intimacy)
- *"Your feedback means the world to us."* (generic flattery)
- Lifecycle emails that express the company's feelings at length before stating the purpose of the email.

### 4.3 Error and Support Copy

**Evidence strength: high — UX writing practitioner consensus, humantone.io analysis, 2025 research.**

Error and support copy is where AI warmth does its most damage. A good error message is clear, specific, non-blaming, and gives a next step. AI generates error copy that apologises extensively, validates the reader's assumed frustration, and then fails to explain what went wrong or what to do about it.[^34][^35][^36]

**Principles for authentic error copy:**
1. **Name what happened, specifically.** *"Your card was declined"* beats *"Something went wrong."*
2. **Give a likely cause.** *"This usually means the billing address doesn't match."*
3. **Give the next step.** One action, not three options that require the reader to diagnose the problem themselves.
4. **Calibrate the apology to the severity.** Minor inconvenience: no apology needed. System failure during a critical period: a specific, first-person, active-voice acknowledgement of what was lost.[^15]
5. **Avoid blame in both directions.** Don't blame the user; don't perform excessive self-flagellation that requires the user to comfort the company.
6. **Active voice.** *"We made the wrong call"* rather than *"The wrong decision was made."*[^15]

**2025 research context:** A 2025 study in *Computers in Human Behavior* found that warm-toned AI apologies generated *more negative emotions* than human-authored ones. The finding supports the practitioner intuition: when AI tonal warmth is detected, it undermines the message rather than softening it.[^37]

**What reads as AI in error copy:**
- *"We sincerely apologise for any inconvenience this may have caused."*
- *"This is not the experience we want for you."*
- *"We're working hard to ensure this doesn't happen again."* (without saying what *this* was or what the work involves)
- Generic error messages that could apply to any failure.

### 4.4 Community and Forum Posts

**Evidence strength: medium — practitioner and community consensus.**

Community and forum registers require more natural rhythm and more permission to be incomplete. Over-polished posts read as corporate intrusions. The conventions of good community writing include:
- Answering the specific question asked, not the question that would be easiest to answer.
- Acknowledging when you don't know or when it's complicated.
- Using the person's name when replying to a specific person's post (once, naturally).
- Avoiding HR-level courtesy: *"Great question!"* is condescending in a community context.
- Matching the register of the community — if people are casual, you can be casual; if they're technical, be precise; don't impose a customer-service sheen on a developer forum.

**What reads as AI in community posts:**
- Replies that open with *"Absolutely!"* or *"Great question!"*
- Every reply having the same structure: acknowledge, explain, offer next steps, sign off warmly.
- Excessive bullet-pointing of information that would naturally be prose.
- The community manager voice that sounds identical on a question about a bug and a question about where to find the community guidelines.

### 4.5 Personal Outreach and Replies

**Evidence strength: medium — practitioner consensus, community observation.**

Personal outreach is where AI tells are most damaging, because the entire point of personal outreach is that it is personal. A message that reads as AI-generated signals that the writer could not be bothered to write — which communicates the inverse of warmth.[^38][^1]

**What reads as authentic:**
- One specific thing that proves you read, watched, or engaged with the person's work.
- A genuine question, not a question designed to open a sales conversation.
- A clear statement of what you want (if you want something) without disguising it as pure interest.
- Short. Two to four sentences for cold outreach. More can feel like an obligation.
- No preamble. *"I hope this finds you well"* is the most detectable hollow courtesy in professional English.[^16]

**The peer test:** Would you write this to a colleague you respect? If the answer is no — if the register is more polished, more formal, or more effusive than you'd use with a peer — revise it down.

## 5. Quick Reference

### 5.1 Kill-List: Words, Phrases, and Patterns to Ban

**Lexical bans (delete on sight):**

| Category | Words/phrases to remove |
|----------|------------------------|
| Reflexive intensifiers | deeply, truly, genuinely, sincerely, absolutely, certainly, incredibly, truly, remarkably, fundamentally |
| The delve family | delve, leverage (as verb), harness, utilise, empower, foster, streamline, robust, innovative |
| Ornate nouns | tapestry, landscape, ecosystem, paradigm, journey, community (as hollow collective), synergy, framework |
| Fake intimacy openers | "Here's the thing", "Here's the kicker", "Here's what most people miss", "Let me be real with you", "Here's an uncomfortable truth" |
| Hollow enthusiasm | amazing, exciting (before it's been earned), incredible, can't wait, so thrilled |
| Dead courtesy phrases | "I hope this finds you well", "As per my last email", "Don't hesitate to reach out", "We appreciate your patience", "We value your feedback" |
| Generic empathy | "We understand how frustrating this must be", "This isn't the experience we want for you", "Your feelings are completely valid", "We know this can be difficult" |
| Passive apology | "Mistakes were made", "Issues were encountered", "This situation occurred" |
| HR-courtesy formulas | "Great question!", "Absolutely!", "Of course!" (as conversation openers), "Happy to help!" |

**Structural bans:**

- Negative parallelism in quantity: *"It's not X, it's Y"* (one per piece maximum)
- Short punchy fragments as emotional staccato: *"We heard you. We acted."*
- The tricolon of empty commitment: *"We're here to help. We're here to listen. We're here for you."*
- The template empathy sequence: validate → paraphrase → pivot → resolve, deployed by reflex
- Exclamation marks in runs of more than one per paragraph
- Emoji as substitute for earned register

**Tonal bans:**

- Grandiose stakes inflation for minor occasions
- False vulnerability (*"I'll be honest…"* followed by the safe option)
- Vague attributions (*"studies show"*, *"experts say"*, *"many users find"*)
- Invented concept labels deployed as if they were established terms

### 5.2 Positive Checklist: Genuine-Warmth Moves

**Earn every expression of warmth with a specific act of it:**

- [ ] **Specificity test:** Can this sentence only refer to this reader, this situation, this moment? If it could have been written for anyone, revise it.
- [ ] **Peer test:** Would you write this to a colleague you respect? If not, it is too formal, too effusive, or too hedge-heavy.
- [ ] **Read-aloud test:** Does it flow in your natural speaking voice? Ogilvy's test still holds.[^29][^30]
- [ ] **Useful-or-performative test:** Does this sentence give the reader something? Or does it perform the giving?
- [ ] **Apology calibration:** Apologise once, specifically, in active voice, for the actual harm. Then address the harm. Do not pre-emptively apologise for anticipated frustration.
- [ ] **Enthusiasm calibration:** Earn each exclamation mark. The more you use, the less each is worth.
- [ ] **Next-step test:** Every error message, support reply, and onboarding message should leave the reader knowing exactly what to do next.
- [ ] **Pronoun ratio:** *You* should appear more often than *we* or *I* in any piece of warmth copy.[^26][^27]
- [ ] **Contraction check:** Use contractions (*we're*, *you'll*, *it's*, *don't*) unless the format requires formal register. Contractions signal human register instantly.[^27]
- [ ] **Active voice for accountability:** When something went wrong, own it in the active voice. *"We broke this"* rather than *"An issue was identified."*
- [ ] **Humour gate:** Is the humour specific to this piece of content? Does it serve the reader's task or interrupt it? If you're unsure, Mailchimp's rule applies: keep a straight face.[^25]
- [ ] **Length check:** Is there a sentence or paragraph whose primary function is to make the writer feel like they've been warm? Cut it. The remaining copy will be warmer.

## Evidence Strength Key

- **High:** Multiple independent sources — peer-reviewed research, established practitioner guides, and practitioner community consensus with documented examples.
- **Medium-high:** Solid practitioner consensus supported by at least one empirical or primary source.
- **Medium:** Practitioner consensus without strong empirical backing; solid enough for practical guidance but open to refinement.

## Notes on Sources and Dating

- *Nicely Said* (Fenton & Kiefer Lee, 2014) remains a primary practitioner reference; its core principles have not been superseded, though some specific format guidance is dated in the context of 2026 AI-generated copy.
- Mailchimp's Content Style Guide is a living document; the voice and tone principles cited here are current as of the 2025 public version.[^39][^25]
- The Shalevska (2024) corpus study used ChatGPT 3.5; hedging patterns in more recent models (GPT-4o, Claude 3.x) may differ in distribution, though practitioners report the smoothed-neutrality pattern persisting.[^5][^40][^4]
- The Microsoft Research empathy template study (2026) is the most recent primary source on the structural formularism of AI empathy; it has not yet been peer-reviewed at the time of this report's preparation.[^14][^3]
- tropes.fyi (launched 2026) is practitioner-built, not peer-reviewed, but is the most comprehensive and current taxonomy of AI writing tells available.[^10][^11]
