# The Persuasive / Market Voice: Authentic vs AI-Generated (2026)
## A Working Reference for Marketing, Landing-Page, Value-Prop and Inbound Copy

*Evidence tags: **[Strong]** = peer-reviewed research, large-sample data, or multi-source cross-validation; **[Moderate]** = practitioner studies, platform data, or small-sample research; **[Anecdotal]** = practitioner observation, single-source claim, or community consensus.*

---

## Executive Summary

In 2026, AI-generated marketing copy has converged on a recognisable register — high surface polish, low information density, reflexive positivity, and a set of structural tics (manufactured contrast, tricolon, em-dash overuse) that readers detect faster than any formal detector can. The trust cost is real: 31% of consumers say visible AI-generated content makes them trust a brand *less*, and only 7% say it makes them trust it *more*. The craft counter-move is not to sound less professional but to apply the century-old principle that specificity carries its own proof: concrete language outperforms vague claims every time. This document catalogues the slop register in full, codifies the authentic craft tradition, gives worked before-and-after examples, and ends with a ready-to-paste kill list and craft checklist.

## Part 1 — The Slop Twin: Cataloguing the AI Marketing Register

### 1.1 What Slop Is (and Why It Exists)

Merriam-Webster named "slop" its 2025 Word of the Year, citing the explosion of low-quality, AI-created digital content. A 2025 academic paper, *Measuring AI "Slop" in Text* (Shaib, Chakrabarty, Garcia-Olano & Wallace; arXiv:2509.19163, submitted to ICLR 2026), provides the first formal taxonomy. Researchers interviewed 19 experts in NLP, writing, linguistics, and philosophy and organised their findings into three themes: **Information Utility** (density, relevance), **Information Quality** (factuality, subjectivity/bias), and **Style Quality** (repetition, templatedness, coherence, verbosity, word complexity, tone). Their key finding: *relevance, density, and tone* are the strongest predictors of a "slop" label. **[Strong]**

The root cause is a feedback loop: AI models are trained on the same corpus of SEO copy, blog posts, LinkedIn articles, and marketing fluff. As AI generates more content and that content enters future training data, the echo chamber intensifies — producing what one practitioner described as a "linguistic monoculture." **[Moderate]**

### 1.3 Structural Tells

#### 1.3a Manufactured-Contrast Aphorisms **[Strong — cross-validated practitioner + detection research]**

The pattern "It's not X, it's Y" (antithetical parallelism) is one of the two most reliably AI-identified structural patterns in marketing copy, alongside anaphora (sentences starting with the same word). The pattern sounds momentarily profound but is recognised as formulaic on repeated exposure. Examples of the tell:

- "Not just fast. Extraordinary."
- "It's not software. It's your competitive advantage."
- "We don't build products. We build futures."
- "This isn't a tool. It's a transformation."

The fix is simple: frame positively with specific evidence instead of stating what something is *not*.

#### 1.3b The Rule-of-Three Syndrome **[Moderate]**

AI defaults to tricolon structures — "Point 1. Point 2. Point 3. Dramatic conclusion." — because training data heavily rewards this pattern. Human writing is measurably more uneven and rhythmically unpredictable. The structural signal is not three items per se (tricolon is a legitimate device) but the *universal application* of three items to every list, every section, every CTA cluster, regardless of whether three is the natural number.

#### 1.3c The Feature-Card Cadence **[Moderate]**

Hero headline → 3-column feature grid → "How it works" 3-step process → 3 testimonials → pricing table → CTA. This layout is now so generic that experienced buyers pattern-match it as "another SaaS homepage." The issue is not any single element but the formulaic assembly and the copy that accompanies it: each feature card gets an emoji, a bold label in three words, and two sentences of benefit language that could describe any competitor.

#### 1.3d Hero-Headline Shape **[Moderate]**

The AI-default hero headline takes the form: **[Action verb] + [audience's pain/goal] + [superlative claim]**. Examples: "Supercharge Your Team's Productivity", "Unlock Unprecedented Growth", "Transform the Way You Work." The tell is the floating vague claim — no number, no product name, no mechanism, no specificity. It could be the headline for any product in any category.

The AI-default subheadline reinforces the pattern: "[Product] is the [superlative] [category] that [empty benefit phrase]." Example: "Acme is the all-in-one platform that empowers teams to do more with less."

#### 1.3e Bullet-Point Bloat **[Moderate]**

AI reliably produces 5–8 bullet points per section, each starting with an action verb and ending with an abstract noun phrase. The bullets are syntactically parallel and semantically interchangeable. Human copy is often deliberately ragged: short punchy line, then a longer elaborated one, then back to short — rhythm as persuasion.

### 1.4 Tonal Tells

#### 1.4a Forced Enthusiasm and Hollow Superlatives **[Strong]**

"Amazing, remarkable, exciting opportunities, captivating, inspiring" — GPTZero's analysis places these in the "emotional/engagement language" bucket that AI over-applies. The tell is not enthusiasm itself but *undifferentiated* enthusiasm: every product claim, every feature, every result is greeted with the same register of excitement. Human writing reserves superlatives for moments when they are earned.

#### 1.4b Fake Scarcity and Reflexive Urgency **[Moderate]**

AI-generated CTAs and email subject lines lean on urgency signals ("Don't miss out," "Last chance," "Limited availability") without establishing any genuine scarcity. Readers have been conditioned to discount these signals. The authentic equivalent is a specific, grounded reason the timing matters: "We're closing beta access on 15 July because the onboarding queue is full" beats "Don't miss out!" in both credibility and conversion.

#### 1.4c Reflexive Positivity / Solution Bias **[Strong — arXiv taxonomy]**

The arXiv slop taxonomy identifies "bias (subjectivity)" as a key dimension: AI marketing copy systematically suppresses negative information, limitation acknowledgements, and realistic qualification. Human persuasive writing earns trust precisely by acknowledging what the product is *not* for. This is not a call for negativity — it is a call for honest framing, which converts better than undifferentiated cheerleading.

#### 1.4d Moral-Discomfort Effect **[Strong — Journal of Business Research]**

A peer-reviewed study (Kirk & Givi, 2024, *Journal of Business Research*) found that when consumers believe emotional messages are AI-generated, they experience *moral discomfort* and rate the brand as manipulative. This is distinct from merely disliking the copy — it triggers an active trust-penalty response, mediated through perceived inauthenticity.

A December 2025 survey of 8,000 consumers by Klaviyo and Datalily (published March 2026) found:
- Only **7%** of consumers say visible AI-generated marketing makes them trust a brand *more*
- **31%** say it makes them trust a brand *less*
- **52%** say they would stop buying from a brand after an inauthentic experience
- **91%** expect brands to disclose when using AI in marketing

NielsenIQ research (2025) found that **64%** of viewers can identify AI-made ads and trust them **25% less** than human-made ones. **[Strong]**

## Part 2 — The Authentic Craft: What Converts Without Slop

### 2.1 The Century-Old Core Principle: Specificity

The single most validated principle in persuasive copywriting, documented across more than a century of direct-response practice, is: **specific, concrete language consistently outperforms vague alternatives.** Examples documented in the direct-response canon:

- "Our lamps give 33% more light" → outperformed "our lamps are brighter" (Claude Hopkins)
- "52.7% higher than U.S. Government standard" → beat "over 50%" (Hopkins)
- "72,000 nerve endings" → beat "many nerve endings" (Hopkins)

The mechanism is simple: vague claims invite scepticism; specific claims carry their own proof. When you write "33% more light," the reader accepts it. When you write "brighter," they think, *says who?* **[Strong]**

This is the precise inverse of the slop register: AI defaults to vagueness because vagueness is safer and easier to generate. Specificity requires actual research, real numbers, genuine customer knowledge.

### 2.2 Voice of Customer (VoC): Let the Buyer Write the Copy

Joanna Wiebe (Copyhackers), the foundational practitioner in conversion copywriting, codifies the process: **copywriting starts with message-finding, not writing.** The method:

1. Mine customer reviews (Amazon, G2, Capterra, Yelp) for exact phrasing customers use to describe the problem your product solves
2. Conduct customer interviews; record and transcribe; listen for the phrases that make you pause
3. Use surveys to surface objections in the prospect's own language
4. Plug VoC language directly into copy — do not paraphrase it

The key insight is that VoC language is not polished. It contains hesitations, colloquialisms, and specific detail. That is exactly what makes it convert: readers recognise their own voice, which builds the fastest form of trust. **[Moderate — practitioner canon, cross-validated by Copyhackers A/B test data]**

### 2.3 Ogilvy's Fundamentals (Still Operative) **[Strong — cross-validated practitioner canon]**

David Ogilvy's principles, distilled to their actionable core:

1. **The headline is 80% of the dollar.** Five times as many people read the headline as read body copy. If the headline fails, the rest is invisible.
2. **Write like you talk, for one person.** Not a stadium — a private letter to one person on behalf of another. Corporate speak is the enemy.
3. **Facts, not fluff, create conviction.** The more specific information you provide about a product, the more likely you are to close the sale.
4. **Promise a benefit.** Headlines must answer: what's in it for me?
5. **Research first.** Ogilvy spent weeks reading product research before writing a single word of the Rolls-Royce ad. Copy is the output of research turned into sentences.

### 2.4 Gary Halbert: Sell Like You're Talking to One Person

Gary Halbert's core contribution to conversion craft: copy must read as a one-on-one conversation, not a broadcast. His practical method — record yourself selling the product aloud to a real prospect, transcribe it, edit it — produces copy that does not sound like AI because it is literally speech compressed. The characteristic register: contractions, sentence fragments used deliberately, direct address ("Here's what I mean"), self-interruptions, parenthetical asides, specific numbers.

Halbert's "by the numbers" principle: when you can replace an adjective with a measurement, always replace it. **[Anecdotal — practitioner canon, primary source: The Gary Halbert Letter]**

### 2.5 Eugene Schwartz: Awareness Levels Determine Copy Shape **[Strong — copywriting canon, widely applied]**

Schwartz's five stages of prospect awareness (*Breakthrough Advertising*, 1966, still current) determine what a headline must do:

| Awareness Stage | What the Prospect Knows | Headline Job |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | No problem consciousness | Name the latent frustration |
| Problem Aware | Knows the pain, not the solution | Amplify the problem, introduce possibility |
| Solution Aware | Knows solutions exist, not yours | Lead with mechanism/category |
| Product Aware | Knows you but hasn't bought | Lead with offer, proof, differentiation |
| Most Aware | Ready to buy | Price, deal, urgency |

The slop register universally addresses a "Most Aware" prospect with outcome promises and CTAs, even when landing at a "Problem Aware" audience. The mismatch is a conversion killer. Authentic copy matches awareness stage. **[Strong]**

### 2.6 Robert Bly and Joseph Sugarman: Mechanics of the Slippery Slide

Robert Bly (*The Copywriter's Handbook*): every element of copy has one job — keep the reader reading. Structure follows reader psychology, not convention. Sub-headlines are not decorative: they are sequential proof points for scanner readers who never read body copy.

Joseph Sugarman's "slippery slide" (*The Adweek Copywriting Handbook*): the headline's job is to get the first sentence read. The first sentence's job is to get the second read. Every sentence earns the next. This creates forward momentum that makes the copy feel conversational rather than promotional — the reader slides rather than steps. **[Moderate — practitioner canon]**

Implications for slop avoidance: AI copy interrupts the slide at every bullet point by introducing a new topic with a new sub-headline. Human copy in the Sugarman tradition uses continuity — each element leads to the next with a logical or curiosity bridge.

### 2.7 Harry Dry (Marketing Examples): Three Tests for Every Sentence **[Anecdotal — practitioner, widely cited]**

Harry Dry's three-question filter for any line of copy:

1. **Can I visualise it?** If you cannot picture it, it is abstract. Abstract does not convert.
2. **Can I prove it true or false?** If neither, it is a superlative. Superlatives do not convert.
3. **Could nobody else write this?** If a competitor could run the same line unchanged, cut it.

The third test is the most diagnostic for slop: AI-generated marketing copy fails test three universally. It is structurally competitor-agnostic.

### 2.8 Readability and Conversion Research **[Strong]**

- Top-converting landing pages write at a **7th-grade reading level** (Flesch-Kincaid). The median page writes at 9th-grade. Every grade level reduction correlates with a **4% increase in conversion rate**.
- A landing page rewritten from grade 14 to grade 7 saw conversions **double** in a documented case study.
- Unbounce research on lead-generation pages found the median conversion rate for 6th-grade copy is **nearly double** that of university-level copy.
- Pages with fewer than 200 words have a **33% higher** median conversion rate than pages with 800+ words.
- Pages with clear, benefit-driven headlines of 6–10 words convert **28% higher** than pages with vague or long headlines.
- The correlation between copy quality and conversion rate is stronger than the correlation between design quality and conversion rate. **[Strong — multiple sources]**

### 2.9 Proof Architecture: Social Proof, Specificity, and Placement

- Authentic, multi-format social proof (reviews and video testimonials) can boost B2B SaaS conversions by up to **270%**. **[Moderate]**
- Place testimonials and credentials near *friction points* — the sections where a prospect is most likely to object or hesitate — not at the bottom of the page.
- Specific testimonials ("It saved us 3 hours a week on reporting" vs. "It's great!") outperform generic ones. The specificity carries the same credibility transfer as specific numbers in body copy.
- Client logos alone move needle; client logos plus a quoted, specific outcome move it further.

## Part 3 — The Boundary: Where Authentic Intensity Tips into Slop

### 3.1 The Core Diagnostic

The difference between authentic persuasive intensity and slop is **specificity density**. High-intensity language earns its place when supported by a concrete referent (a number, a named person, a documented outcome, a sensory detail). High-intensity language without a concrete referent is slop regardless of how polished it sounds.

Secondary diagnostic: **who could say this?** If the copy could appear unchanged on a competitor's page, it has crossed into slop territory. Authentic copy is always, in some sense, indelibly yours — it contains evidence that only you could produce.

### 3.2 Worked Before-and-After Examples

---

#### 3.2a Hero Copy

**Slop version:**
> *Supercharge Your Team's Productivity*
> Acme is the all-in-one platform that empowers teams to do more with less — unlocking unprecedented efficiency and transformative results. Start your free trial today.

**Why it fails:** Generic action verb (supercharge), undifferentiated category claim (all-in-one), empty intensifiers (unprecedented, transformative), vague benefit (do more with less). Could be any product.

**Authentic version:**
> *Close your reporting tab. The numbers update themselves.*
> Acme pulls live data from Salesforce, HubSpot, and your data warehouse — and builds the board deck for you. Our customers cut weekly reporting from 4 hours to 20 minutes.
> *Try it free for 14 days.*

**Why it works:** Specific pain (reporting tab, board deck), specific mechanism (named integrations), specific outcome (4 hours to 20 minutes), single concrete CTA.

---

#### 3.2b Value Proposition

**Slop version:**
> *Revolutionising the way modern teams work.*
> We deliver world-class, enterprise-grade solutions that leverage cutting-edge technology to drive meaningful impact across your organisation.

**Why it fails:** No single concrete claim, no category, no audience, no proof, no differentiator. Could be a law firm, an HR tool, or a coffee supplier.

**Authentic version:**
> *The CRM built for 10-person sales teams, not Fortune 500 IT departments.*
> No implementation consultant. No 6-month onboarding. Most teams are running live calls through Acme within the first afternoon.

**Why it works:** Specific audience (10-person teams), specific negative differentiator (not Fortune 500), specific proof of speed (first afternoon).

---

#### 3.2c CTA

**Slop version:**
> *Don't miss out on our transformative platform. Get started today and unlock your team's full potential!*

**Why it fails:** Fake urgency, hollow superlative, exclamation-mark enthusiasm that signals desperation.

**Authentic version:**
> *Start your 14-day free trial — no credit card, no onboarding call required.*

Or:

> *See your first report in 20 minutes — or we'll set it up for you.*

**Why it works:** Specific (14 days, no credit card), removes friction, the guarantee is concrete not vague.

---

#### 3.2d Case Study

**Slop version:**
> *Acme helped TechCorp Solutions achieve remarkable results, driving meaningful impact across their sales organisation. The team experienced transformative improvements in productivity and saw significant growth in their key metrics.*

**Why it fails:** No numbers, no named people, no before-state, no mechanism. "Significant growth" and "meaningful impact" are non-claims.

**Authentic version:**
> *Phoebe Walsh, Head of Revenue at Northgate (47 people, £4M ARR), was spending 6 hours every Friday building the weekly pipeline report. Two weeks after switching to Acme, that job automated. She now uses the time for deal reviews.*
> *Northgate's pipeline visibility improved enough that they caught a churning account in time to save the renewal — £85k.*

**Why it works:** Named person with title, company size and ARR context, specific time saved (6 hours), specific outcome (£85k renewal saved), timeline (two weeks). Every claim is checkable or imageable.

---

#### 3.2e Cold Outreach

**Slop version:**
> *Hi [Name], I hope this email finds you well. I wanted to reach out because I've been following your incredible journey at [Company] and I'm really impressed by your vision. At Acme, we help innovative companies like yours leverage the power of AI to supercharge growth. I'd love to explore potential synergies and schedule a quick 15-minute call.*

**Why it fails:** "Hope this finds you well" is the most reliable spam trigger in 2026. "Incredible journey," "leverage," "supercharge," "potential synergies" are instant delete signals. No evidence of research.

**Authentic version:**
> *Tom — saw you're hiring two SDRs on LinkedIn (both roles posted last week). A lot of sales teams at your growth stage spend the first 90 days training reps on outreach cadence from scratch.*
> *We built a tool that gives new reps a working sequence on day one, informed by what's actually converting for your ICP. Happy to show you a 10-minute demo.*
> *Worth a look?*

**Why it works:** Specific trigger (LinkedIn job posting, last week), specific pain (90-day ramp), specific mechanism, specific ask (10-minute demo), no fake enthusiasm.

The following gradient maps authentic copy energy against slop risk:

| Register | Example | Risk Level |
|---|---|---|

## Part 4 — 2026 Register Guidance by Format

### 4.1 Landing Pages: Overall Principles **[Strong/Moderate]**

The most common structural mistake in 2026 landing pages is *abstract visual storytelling* in the hero paired with *vague transformation language*. AI-generated copy leads with outcomes ("transform", "revolutionise", "unlock") before establishing the specific problem, audience, and mechanism. Human copy earns the outcome claim by establishing credibility first.

Evidence-based structure:
1. **Hero:** What is it? Why should I care? What do I do? (answerable in under 5 seconds)
2. **Problem framing:** Name the specific frustration in the customer's language
3. **Mechanism:** How does it actually work — show the interface, name the method
4. **Proof:** Specific testimonials and numbers, placed at friction points
5. **CTA:** Repeated with context at logical decision points, not just at the bottom

Grade level target: 6–8 Flesch-Kincaid. Run your copy through Hemingway Editor before publishing.

### 4.2 Hero Sections

**Authentic tells:**
- Headline answers a specific question or names a specific problem
- Sub-headline identifies the mechanism or audience
- CTA describes what happens next (not just "Start free trial" — "See your first report in 20 minutes")
- Social proof is visible in the hero (number of customers, a logo strip, a specific data point)

**Slop tells:**
- Headline is a floating transformation promise
- Sub-headline stacks two more vague claims
- CTA is "Get Started" or "Try for Free" with no friction-removal
- The hero image is abstract/conceptual rather than showing the actual product in use

### 4.3 Pricing Copy

Pricing copy is where AI-generated text most visibly fails: it defaults to hollow adjectives for tier names ("Basic / Professional / Enterprise") and generic bullet lists for feature differences. Authentic pricing copy:

- Uses tier names that describe the buyer, not the product level ("Solo operator / Growing team / Scaling company")
- States the specific outcome the price buys, not just the feature ("Build unlimited reports" vs. "Unlimited access")
- Handles objections explicitly ("No credit card. Cancel any time. Seriously." beats unstated assumptions)
- Shows the cost of *not* buying — specific, time-based ("At 3 hours of reporting time per week, that's £4,000/year in senior-employee hours")

### 4.4 Case Studies

The AI-generated case study template is: Company Background → Challenge (vague) → Solution (feature-list) → Results (percentages without baseline). It converts poorly because every element is abstract.

Authentic case study structure:
1. **Named protagonist with context** (person, role, company size, ARR)
2. **Specific before-state** (what exactly were they doing, how long did it take, what was breaking)
3. **Decision moment** (why did they look for a solution, what triggered the search)
4. **Mechanism** (specifically what the product did, not just "our platform")
5. **Specific outcomes with timeline** (not "significant improvements" — "£85k renewal saved in week two")
6. **Quote in the customer's actual voice** (rough-edged, specific, not marketing-polished)

### 4.5 Founder / About Page Copy **[Moderate]**

The About page is becoming a strategic asset in 2026 — not just origin story but culture-building and buyer reassurance. AI-generated About pages are uniformly structured as: founding story → mission statement → values (three bullet points) → team photos. Authentic About copy:

- Leads with *why you built this* in terms that reveal a genuine frustration, not a market opportunity
- Uses the founder's actual voice with all its idiosyncrasies — first-person contractions, a sentence fragment, an opinion that could be wrong
- Names something the company won't do (as important as what it will)
- Gives specific detail: the year, the city, the customer they had in mind
- Includes one genuine imperfection or moment of doubt — this is the single strongest authenticity signal available

**Slop About page:**
> *We're a passionate team of innovators dedicated to transforming the way businesses leverage technology. Our mission is to empower organisations to unlock their full potential through cutting-edge solutions.*

**Authentic About page:**
> *I built this because I was tired of rebuilding the same dashboard in every sales job I had. It took me six months to convince the first twenty companies it was real. Some of them are still customers.*

### 4.6 Short-Form Social and Outreach Copy

In 2026, over 40% of all cold email traffic is AI-generated, producing a "delete reflex" among B2B buyers. Response rates for cold email have dropped to 2.1% overall, though top performers achieve 10.7%+ reply rates through genuine personalisation.

**Authentic social and outreach register:**
- Specific trigger ("Saw your post about [specific claim] last Tuesday")
- Single observation, not a pitch ("Curious how you handle [specific problem]")
- Short — under 80 words for cold email
- No exclamation marks, no "hope this finds you well," no "I'd love to connect"
- One clear, low-friction ask ("Worth a look?" or "Would a 10-min demo be useful?")

**What converts in 2026 social copy (vs. what reads as AI):**
- A specific number that surprises ("83% of our users deleted their previous tool within a week")
- An opinion that could be disagreed with ("Most CRMs are built for managers, not the people using them")
- A reference to something real and recent (a news story, a platform change, a common complaint)
- Sentence fragments used for rhythm: deliberate, not accidental

## Part 5 — Quick Reference

### 5.1 The Kill List: Buzzwords and Slop Patterns to Ban

#### Banned Verbs
leverage, empower, supercharge, revolutionise, disrupt, unlock, streamline, foster, elevate, transform (as an intransitive goal), optimise (when vague), scale (as a standalone aspiration), accelerate, amplify, pioneer, cultivate, illuminate, navigate, converge, propel, synthesise

#### Banned Adjectives and Noun Clusters
world-class, enterprise-grade, best-in-class, cutting-edge, groundbreaking, game-changing, holistic, robust (when used for "thorough"), seamless, dynamic, transformative, comprehensive (when vague), scalable and sustainable, visionary, innovative (without evidence), purpose-driven, human-centric, unparalleled, unprecedented, remarkable

#### Banned Opener and Closer Phrases
- "In today's fast-paced digital world…"
- "A new [X] is quietly emerging…"
- "At the forefront of innovation…"
- "We're redefining what's possible…"
- "Not just [X]. [Vaguer X]."
- "It's not [X], it's [Y]." (antithetical parallelism)
- "[X], [X], [X]." (tricolon with identical abstract nouns)
- "Hope this finds you well"
- "I'd love to connect"
- "Let's explore potential synergies"
- "Authentic and relatable stories"
- "Driving meaningful impact"
- "Delivering unparalleled value"
- "Taking a deep dive"
- "In conclusion" / "To summarise" / "Ultimately"
- "It is important to note that"
- "Crucially" / "Importantly" / "Notably"

#### Banned Structural Patterns
- Manufactured-contrast aphorism as headline ("Not just reports. Clarity.")
- Universal tricolon (three items on every list regardless of natural number)
- Feature-card bullet bloat (5–8 bullets per section, all syntactically parallel)
- Abstract visual + transformation headline in hero (no product shown, no mechanism named)
- Generic tier names: Basic / Professional / Enterprise
- Case study results without baseline ("significant improvements", "meaningful impact")
- Testimonials without specifics ("It changed everything!" — named person, no detail)

#### Punctuation and Rhythm Flags
- Em-dash overuse: more than 1–2 per paragraph is automation, not craft
- Stacked em-dashes in hero or value-prop copy specifically (the pattern: "X — the platform that — does Y")
- Exclamation marks in B2B copy
- Sentence-ending intensifiers: "truly," "really," "genuinely" (especially in marketing claims)

---

### 5.2 The Positive Checklist: Craft Moves to Apply

#### Research First
- [ ] Mine 20+ customer reviews (G2, Capterra, Amazon, Yelp) for exact problem-description language
- [ ] Conduct at least one customer interview; transcribe; extract verbatim phrases
- [ ] List every fact, measurement, and specific outcome for the product before writing
- [ ] Identify which Schwartz awareness stage the landing page is targeting

#### Specificity Moves
- [ ] Replace every adjective-claim with a measurement where possible (not "faster" — "saves 3 hours/week")
- [ ] Name the audience specifically, not generically ("solo freelance designers", not "teams")
- [ ] Name the before-state with a concrete scenario (day, time, what breaks)
- [ ] Name the mechanism, not just the outcome ("pulls live data from Salesforce and builds the deck" vs. "automates reporting")
- [ ] Give case study results with baseline + timeline + named person

#### Harry Dry's Three-Test Filter (every line)
- [ ] Can I visualise it?
- [ ] Can I prove it true or false?
- [ ] Could nobody else write this?

#### Voice and Rhythm Moves
- [ ] Read the copy aloud — if it would sound odd in a real conversation, rewrite it
- [ ] Use contractions (it's, you'll, we've, they're) — AI defaults to formal constructions
- [ ] Vary sentence length deliberately: short → long → very short → medium
- [ ] Use one deliberate fragment for rhythm ("Not because it's clever. Because it works.")
- [ ] Include at least one specific sensory or time-bound detail ("5pm on a Friday," "the third deck revision in a week")

#### Proof Architecture
- [ ] Place testimonials next to the claim they support, not in a separate "testimonials" section
- [ ] Testimonials must contain: named person + role + company + specific outcome
- [ ] Include at least one number in the hero section (customers, time saved, money saved, percentage)
- [ ] CTA copy removes friction explicitly ("no credit card," "cancel any time," "set up in 20 minutes")
- [ ] Pricing copy states what the buyer is buying, not just what features they get

#### Structural Checks
- [ ] Hero: answers "what is this / why care / what next" in under 5 seconds
- [ ] Readability grade: 6–8 (run through Hemingway Editor)
- [ ] Word count: if over 800 words on a lead-gen page, cut until it isn't
- [ ] No "It's not X, it's Y" constructions
- [ ] No manufactured tricolon — if you have three items, ask whether there are actually four or two
- [ ] Cold email under 80 words; one specific trigger; one low-friction ask

## Appendix: Evidence Notes and Caveats

**What has dated:**
- Unbounce's original Flesch-Kincaid / conversion rate data comes from circa 2017 but has been corroborated by 2026 analysis. The direction of the finding (simpler = better) is robust; the specific percentage figures should be treated as indicative rather than precise.
- Gary Halbert's and David Ogilvy's principles derive from print direct-response (1960s–1990s). The mechanisms (specificity, proof, VoC language) are validated by modern A/B test data. Specific format advice (long copy vs. short) is context-dependent in 2026 and should be tested.

**What is strongest:**
- The trust-penalty data (Klaviyo/Datalily, NielsenIQ, academic studies 2024–2026) on AI-generated content is recent, large-sample, and consistent across cultures and methodologies.
- The arXiv slop taxonomy (Shaib et al., 2025) is the first formal academic framework and is peer-reviewed (ICLR 2026 submission).
- The stylometric fingerprint research (UCC, 2025) confirming AI writing is stylistically distinguishable from human writing is published and cross-validated.
- GPTZero corpus data (3M+ comparisons) on over-used AI vocabulary is the largest available empirical source on the specific lexical tells.

**Known gaps:**
- No large-scale A/B test data (as of June 2026) directly comparing identical products with AI-slop vs. authentic-craft landing pages controlling for all other variables. The conversion advantage of authentic copy is inferred from readability studies, VoC practitioner data, and trust-penalty consumer research rather than from a single clean experiment.
- The precise conversion impact of individual slop patterns (e.g., manufactured contrast vs. hollow superlatives) is not independently quantified in published research.
