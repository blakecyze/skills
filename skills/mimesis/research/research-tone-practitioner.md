# The Practitioner Voice: Writing with Earned Authority in 2026

*A working reference for case studies, portfolio writeups, technical posts, bio copy, and talk abstracts.*

***

## Overview

In 2026 the web is saturated with AI-generated "expert" content that shares a recognisable register: confident in tone, empty of substance, polished to the point of friction-lessness. A University College Cork stylometric study published in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* (2025) confirmed that even the most advanced LLMs — GPT-4, Llama 70B — produce "compact, predictable styles" that cluster tightly together, while human writing "sprawls" with idiosyncratic habits and lived experience. An MIT experiment found that writing produced with LLM assistance converged on the same vocabulary and concepts, with researchers describing a homogenising effect across participants. Separately, a 2026 arXiv study constituted "one of the first empirical demonstrations that co-writing with LLMs induces content homogenisation".[^1][^2][^3]

The result is a paradox: as AI-assisted writing becomes higher quality by surface metrics (grammar, structure, coherence), it becomes less credible as a signal of genuine expertise. Press Gazette analysis in early 2026 found that roughly 10% of quoted "experts" in popular UK journalism titles did not exist at all — AI-generated personas. Readers are adapting: 60% of users now default to scepticism when consuming thought leadership content online, especially when authorship or expertise is unclear.[^4][^5]

This reference document provides specific, repeatable moves for writing that reads as genuinely practitioner-authored.

## 1. The Slop Twin: Cataloguing the AI Authority-Cosplay Register

**Evidence strength:** [Strong] for lexical and structural patterns — drawn from Wikipedia's WikiProject AI Cleanup (15,000-word field guide, thousands of tagged submissions); TechCrunch, The Atlantic, Lit Hub, and Ars Technica coverage of same; peer-reviewed stylometric and corpus research.[^6][^7][^8][^9][^1]

### 1.1 Lexical Tells

The hallmark is **abstraction-heavy, content-free vocabulary** — words that sound authoritative while committing to nothing specific.

**The AI word-hoard (dead giveaways in clusters):**

- *Landscape* ("in today's ever-evolving landscape," "the rapidly shifting landscape of…") — the ultimate non-sentence[^10][^11]
- *Delve, foster, navigate, harness, unlock, leverage, empower, underscore, underscore, elevate, enhance* — the LinkedIn guru vocabulary[^12][^13]
- *Pivotal, crucial, testament, cornerstone, game-changer, paradigm* — empty gravitas[^12][^10]
- *Tapestry, tapestry of, intricate interplay, myriad, treasure trove, realm of* — ornate filler[^13][^10]
- *Synergy, ecosystem, holistic, transformative, cutting-edge, innovative, disruptive* — technology-flavoured nothing[^11]
- *Delve, pave the way, shed light on, resonate with, supercharge, propel to the forefront* — motion metaphors that move nothing[^10]

A single em dash does not prove AI authorship; a cluster of the above words with an em dash most likely does. Wikipedia's AI Cleanup editors note that these words appear because LLMs "sand down specific, unusual, nuanced facts (which are statistically rare) and replace them with more generic, positive descriptions (which are statistically common)" — leaving "a blurry, generic sketch".[^14][^11]

**Sentence-level tells:**

- *In today's fast-paced world / in today's ever-evolving landscape* — the canonical AI opener[^11][^10]
- *In a world where… / In an era characterised by…* — mock-epic framing[^10]
- *Not X, but Y / Not a destination but a journey* — negative parallelisms[^15][^12]
- *Rule-of-threes adjective stacking*: "innovative, transformative, and groundbreaking"[^12]
- *False ranges*: "from intimate gatherings to global movements" — implying a spectrum without specifying any point on it[^15][^12]
- Em dashes used as confetti — AI text runs 30%+ of sentences with em dashes versus 5-10% in human writing[^16]

### 1.2 Structural Tells

**The dutiful five-beat essay.** AI defaults to introduction → three points → conclusion, often with rigidly labelled sections ("Challenges," "Future Prospects," "Key Takeaways") that appear whether warranted or not. Wikipedia editors flag this as one of the most reliable structural signals.[^7][^17][^14]

**Compulsive summaries.** Text that has just said something immediately restates it: *"Overall," "In summary," "In conclusion," "To recap."* Human practitioners, especially in shorter professional writing, end on the substance; AI appends the bow.[^14][^12]

**Listicle scaffolding over argument.** Excessive bulleting, bolding of phrases as though every sentence is a textbook key term, and conversion of continuous reasoning into disconnected list items. The Wikipedia guide specifically flags "excessive use of boldface, lists, or title case in headings" as structural AI tells.[^7][^12]

**Vague tailing clauses with present participles.** Constructions like *"emphasising the significance of…"* or *"reflecting the continued relevance of…"* appended to clauses that have already made a point. TechCrunch's analysis of the Wikipedia guide calls these out as particularly hard to spot but, once seen, impossible to unsee.[^17]

**Claim without evidence.** Statements that sound precise but contain no specifics: *"Research shows that…"* (which research?), *"Many experts argue…"* (which experts?), *"Studies suggest…"* (which studies?). The Wikipedia guide flags *"superficial or vague analyses and attributions"* — e.g. *"some critics argue"* — as a reliable tell.[^7]

### 1.3 Tonal Tells

**Over-hedging in the wrong places.** A corpus-based study published in *Knowledge – International Journal* (2024) analysed 100 ChatGPT essays against 100 human essays and found AI shows "high frequency use of some hedges (such as 'may') and no use of boosters (such as 'clearly', 'definitely', 'certainly') in comparison to human texts which tend to use a wider array of hedges and boosters, more frequently". A 2025 follow-up study in the *Journal of English Studies in Arabia Felix* confirmed that AI "overgeneralises cautious patterns learned from training data to contexts that call for assertive instructional guidance," creating "inappropriate hedging in foundational content where confident presentation would better support learning". Human experts hedge selectively and boost when warranted; AI hedges uniformly and boosts rarely.[^9][^18][^19][^6]

**False humility.** Performed modesty that does not cost anything — *"I'm not an expert, but…"* followed by confident assertions, or *"this is just my perspective"* followed by a bulleted list of Five Principles. Genuine humility names specific limits: *"I only ran this on Postgres 14; I don't know how it behaves on Aurora."*

**Manufactured contrarianism.** The pattern *"Most people think X. But actually, Y"* where Y is only mildly different from X, or where the "most people" is invented. AI generates this structure because contrarianism signals independent thinking without requiring a genuinely original point.[^15]

**Servile positivity.** The Wikipedia guide notes that LLMs default toward generic positive descriptions — everything is notable, significant, unique, or impactful. This produces what one observer called the "TV commercial transcript": landscapes are always scenic, views always breathtaking, everything clean and modern.[^17][^14]

**Expertise asserted, not shown.** Declarations of authority (*"As a seasoned practitioner…," "Drawing on years of experience…"*) that precede generic claims rather than specific observations. Real expertise shows itself through the details chosen, not through CV-prefacing.

**Expertise scope creep.** Forbes contributing writer Rhea Wessel coined the term in November 2025 for the phenomenon where AI enables practitioners to publish "authoritatively" outside their domain, "gradually shifting from depth to volume". Readers are increasingly able to detect when claimed authority does not match the specificity of what is said.[^20][^21]

## 2. The Authentic Craft: What Makes Practitioner Writing Credible

**Evidence strength:** [Strong] for practitioner canon (Paul Graham essays, Patrick McKenzie writing, Julian Shapiro handbook, Stripe writing culture — all primary sources); [Moderate] for rhetorical research (Aristotle's ethos framework, corroborated by multiple academic sources); [Moderate] for 2025-2026 practitioner claims research.

### 2.1 The Rhetorical Foundation: Earned Ethos

Aristotle argued in the *Rhetoric* that "persuasion is achieved by the speaker's personal character when the speech is so spoken as to make us think him credible" and that ethos "should be achieved by what the speaker says" — not by prior reputation. This is the key insight: credibility is demonstrated through the content, not declared before it. Ethos is built in writing through:[^22]

- **Demonstrated knowledge**: showing the reader something specific and true they would not have thought of
- **Intellectual honesty**: acknowledging limits, disagreements, and alternative interpretations[^23][^24]
- **Respect for the reader**: not wasting their time on what they already know[^25]

Aristotle considered ethos "almost the most effective means of persuasion" — more reliable than logical argument alone, because it creates a foundation of trust that makes subsequent arguments credible.[^26][^22]

### 2.2 Specificity as the Primary Move

The single most reliable distinguisher between practitioner and AI-generated expert writing is **specificity** — concrete numbers, named tools, particular conditions, exact dates.

Paul Graham's advice: write "using ordinary words and simple sentences... if you say nothing simply, it will be obvious to everyone, including you". His essays achieve authority not through vocabulary but through unusual precision of observation — what one analyst calls "specific and poetic" phrasing that makes judgements sound *emphatic* rather than generic.[^27][^25]

Technical writing guidance at the sentence level is direct: replace *"the contractor needs to adjust the weight a bit"* with *"the contractor needs to adjust the weight by 5 lb"*. Replace *"to excel in college, you will need to work really hard"* with *"to excel in college, you will need to study at least 30 hours a week"*.[^28]

Patrick McKenzie (patio11) exemplifies specificity in practitioner blogging. His work at Stripe is described as combining "systems thinking applied to businesses" with granular operational detail — annual reviews that named actual numbers, failures alongside wins, and exact technical mechanisms. His writing is cited as a model by practitioners because it reads as someone who has *done* the work rather than someone who has thought about what doing the work might be like.[^29][^30][^31][^32]

**The specificity test:** Can this sentence be falsified? If yes, it probably has some epistemic content. If no (e.g. "AI is transforming the way we work"), it is content-free.

### 2.3 Showing the Work and the Dead Ends

Austin Kleon's *Show Your Work!* argues that the process — including the messy, unfinished, wrong-headed steps — is as important as the polished product. "Think process, not product" and "share something small every day" are principles for building a credible track record through visible work.[^33][^34][^35][^36]

The practitioner canon is consistent on this point:

- Name what you tried that did not work and why
- Show the intermediate state, not just the final result
- Acknowledge the conditions under which your approach breaks down

This is structurally impossible for AI to fake because it requires actual experience of a specific failure. When the Wikipedia guide notes that AI "sands down specific, unusual, nuanced facts," the dead ends are exactly what gets sanded away.[^14]

Stripe's writing culture, described by former employee Brie Wolfson and extensively documented, formalises this as organisational practice: retrospectives are written, not just discussed; kickoff memos name risks and assumptions, not just goals; decisions are documented with their reasoning, including what was rejected and why.[^37][^38][^39]

### 2.4 Naming Real Tradeoffs

Confident, earned authority **names the conditions under which a recommendation breaks down**. This is the structural inverse of AI authority-cosplay, which presents conclusions without costs. A practitioner who has actually used a tool knows when it fails.

Examples of the move:
- *"This approach works well when your data is small enough to fit in memory. At scale, the sort becomes the bottleneck."*
- *"The pattern held across five clients — until we tried it on a regulated industry, where the compliance overhead ate the efficiency gain."*
- *"I'd recommend this for greenfield projects. Don't retrofit it into an existing codebase unless you have six months and very patient stakeholders."*

Julian Shapiro's *Writing Well Handbook* frames this as the key to resonance: authentic writing that "acknowledges the complexity beneath the surface". His formula treats writing quality as Novelty × Resonance — both require truth that the reader does not already have.[^40][^41][^42]

### 2.5 Earned Opinion vs. Asserted Opinion

Ethos requires distinguishing between opinions that have been earned through experience and opinions that are asserted through confidence. The practitioner move is to **ground opinions in observable evidence from their own work**:

- Weak: *"Documentation is crucial for team velocity."*
- Strong: *"We doubled deployment frequency after we required every PR to include a one-paragraph explanation of what breaks if this goes wrong. That cost us about 15 minutes per PR and saved roughly two incidents a week."*

Paul Graham's dictum is: "expect 80% of the ideas in an essay to happen after you start writing it, and 50% of those you start with to be wrong". This is a recipe for genuine discovery through writing — the opposite of fitting pre-formed claims into an essay structure.[^43]

### 2.6 Burstiness and Rhythm

Stylometric research consistently finds that AI writing lacks "burstiness" — the natural variation between very short, punchy sentences and longer, clause-heavy constructions that characterises human prose. The UCC study found AI models produce "tightly grouped clusters" of sentence length and rhythm, while human texts show "far greater variation and individuality".[^44][^1]

The practical move is intentional rhythm variation:
- Short declarative sentence for emphasis
- Followed by a longer sentence that adds qualification or context, showing the thinking
- Return to short to land

Paul Graham uses this extensively — "self-riffing" by referencing his own language, chatty emphasis with *very*, double questions — to create forward motion that rewards sentence-by-sentence reading rather than scanning.[^27]

### 2.7 Stripe, Basecamp, and the Writing-First Culture

Two of the most consistently cited models for high-quality practitioner writing culture in tech are Stripe and Basecamp.[^38][^45][^39]

**Stripe's writing culture** (documented by Brie Wolfson, corroborated by Patrick McKenzie's description of Stripe as "a celebration of the written word"): pre-meeting memos mandatory before any discussion; no brainstorming before writing; retrospectives written not just discussed; kickoff memos name goals, risks, *and assumptions*; Patrick Collison's internal communications known for "sharp reasoning" not just functional information.[^37][^38]

**Basecamp's model**: Jason Fried and DHH built a culture where writing substitutes for meeting wherever possible, and where clarity of written argument is itself a form of thinking rather than a record of thinking that happened elsewhere. Their public-facing writing (Rework, Signal vs. Noise blog) demonstrates the "disagreement and specificity" they value internally — they consistently name what they think is wrong with conventional approaches, with reasons.[^46][^47]

The common thread: writing is doing the thinking, not reporting it. That orientation produces text that reads differently from text that reports conclusions reached elsewhere.

## 3. The Boundary: Authentic Authority vs. Puffery and False Modesty

**Evidence strength:** [Moderate] — derived from practitioner canon and rhetorical principles; before/after examples are constructed from observed patterns with reference to cited sources.

### 3.1 Where Authority Tips into Puffery

Puffery is authority asserted without evidence. It appears in three forms:

1. **Credential-leading**: *"As someone who has spent fifteen years in the industry…"* — the credential does no epistemic work. The claim that follows is not improved by the sentence before it.
2. **Scale inflation**: *"Having worked with hundreds of clients across dozens of industries…"* — impressively vague. The interesting detail would be which client, which industry, what happened.
3. **Adjective-stacking the self**: *"I'm a passionate, results-driven professional committed to delivering transformative outcomes."* — every word is a gesture toward a quality rather than evidence of it.

### 3.2 Where Modesty Tips into Noise

False modesty is the inverse: performed uncertainty that reads as either dishonest or uninformative.

1. **Reflexive disclaimer before confident advice**: *"I'm no expert, but I think you should probably consider restructuring your entire data model."* — pick one register.
2. **Hedging established knowledge**: Applying *"may," "might," "could possibly"* to facts that are well established wastes the reader's trust budget on things that do not warrant uncertainty.[^9]
3. **The non-answer**: Responding to a direct question with *"it depends"* without specifying what it depends on.

The line is: **hedge selectively and specifically, boost when warranted**. Practitioners who have actually done the work know when something is reliable and when it is not. That knowledge — expressed as calibrated confidence — is the credibility signal.

### 3.3 Before / After: Four Formats

#### Case Study Opening

❌ **Slop twin:**
> "In today's fast-paced digital landscape, organisations are increasingly challenged by the need to leverage data-driven insights to unlock competitive advantage. Our client, a leading enterprise in the technology sector, was no exception. They faced significant pain points around scalability and operational efficiency, and they reached out to us to help them navigate these complexities and deliver transformative results."

✅ **Practitioner voice:**
> "The client's reports were taking 14 hours to run. Their analysts were scheduling them overnight and discovering errors at 9 a.m. By the time we finished, the same reports ran in 22 minutes. Here is what we changed — and what we tried first that did not work."

The practitioner version opens with a falsifiable number, names the actual problem, promises to show failures, and drops the decorative framing entirely.

***

#### Project Result

❌ **Slop twin:**
> "The project delivered significant improvements across key metrics, resulting in a meaningful uplift in performance and a positive impact on stakeholder outcomes. The transformative approach we adopted enabled the team to unlock new efficiencies and deliver value at scale."

✅ **Practitioner voice:**
> "Deployment time dropped from 45 minutes to 8 minutes. We reduced failed deployments from roughly one in five to one in thirty over the following quarter. The main change was replacing our shell-script pipeline with a proper dependency graph — the lesson being that the complexity we thought we were avoiding in tooling was just hiding in incident logs."

Numbers, a named mechanism, an honest lesson, and no adjectives doing work that evidence should do.

***

#### About-Page Bio

❌ **Slop twin:**
> "Jane is a passionate and results-driven software architect with over a decade of experience delivering innovative, scalable solutions across multiple industries. A thought leader in cloud-native technologies, she is deeply committed to empowering teams to achieve their full potential and drive transformative change."

✅ **Practitioner voice:**
> "Jane builds data infrastructure at fintech scale. She spent eight years at [Company], where she rebuilt the payments reconciliation system twice — once wrong, once less wrong. She writes about what she learned the second time. Previously at [Company B] and [University]. Based in Edinburgh."

Specific, active, honest about imperfection, and readable in one pass.

***

#### Technical Claim

❌ **Slop twin:**
> "It is important to note that, while this approach may offer certain advantages in specific contexts, there are also potential drawbacks that should be carefully considered by practitioners seeking to implement solutions in their particular environments."

✅ **Practitioner voice:**
> "This works reliably on datasets under 10GB. Above that, the join starts spilling to disk and you'll want a columnar store instead. We hit the wall at around 8GB in production and switched to DuckDB — that solved it for us, though we haven't tested on anything larger than 40GB."

Specific threshold, named alternative, honest scope limit, no hedge tax on well-understood ground.

## 4. 2026 Register Guidance by Format

**Evidence strength:** [Moderate] — derived from practitioner canon, conference abstract guidance from professional bodies, and portfolio/case study craft sources; extrapolated to the 2026 AI-saturation context.

### 4.1 Case Studies (Problem → What You Did → Result)

The format that AI most thoroughly destroys is the case study, because its three-part structure maps perfectly onto AI's five-beat essay default. The slop version: *bloated problem statement, vague methodology, metric-free result, LinkedIn-shaped lesson*.

**Credibility moves:**

- **Open with the number, not the context**: the reader wants to know what changed before they want to know why it mattered
- **Name the wrong turns**: what you tried before the thing that worked, and why it did not work — this is structurally un-fakeable[^48]
- **Use exact metrics with their conditions**: not *"performance improved significantly"* but *"p95 latency dropped from 340ms to 90ms under a load of 500 concurrent users"*
- **Quote the actual client language** if possible — verbatim quotes that carry specific detail do more credibility work than any summary[^48]
- **Name the constraint**: what this approach requires to work, and where it breaks down

60% of thought leadership readers now default to scepticism; the proof economy rewards documentation over declaration. Case studies that name what was tried and failed perform better on credibility than those that present a smooth arc from problem to solution.[^4]

### 4.2 Portfolio Project Writeups

Hiring managers screen portfolios for three signals: relevant work done recently, quality sufficient for their environment, and evidence of how you approach decisions and tradeoffs.[^49]

**Credibility moves:**

- Lead with the **actual technical decision**, not the context-setting: *"I chose X over Y because of Z constraint"* rather than *"This project aimed to deliver a scalable solution"*
- **Show the reasoning**, not just the conclusion: what alternatives you considered and ruled out, with reasons
- **Be specific about your own contribution** if the work was collaborative: *"I owned the API design; the data modelling was joint"*
- **Name the failure or the thing you would change**: *"If I were doing this again, I'd instrument earlier — we spent two days debugging what turned out to be a 3-line configuration error"*
- **Avoid the summary paragraph**: if the writeup has done its job, the reader does not need a paragraph that restates what they just read

### 4.3 Technical Blog Posts

The engineering blog post earns authority through **teaching something the reader could not have found in a five-minute search**. That requires either novel synthesis of public information, or — better — an observation from specific experience.[^50]

**Credibility moves:**

- **Open with the decision, not the background**: readers of technical blogs want to know what to do and why before they want the history of the problem space
- **Name your specific environment**: what version, what scale, what constraints — this limits your claims to what you actually know, and signals honesty
- **Show the code or the configuration or the data** — not paraphrased summaries of it
- **Acknowledge where your advice does not apply**: *"This works for read-heavy workloads; I have not tested it under write contention"*
- **Avoid burstiness-killing list inflation**: if you find yourself converting a paragraph of reasoning into a bulleted list because it looks cleaner, check whether the reasoning has been lost in the conversion

The Stripe writing culture is instructive: engineers are encouraged to write often and informally, not just for major outcomes. Short posts that document a specific, narrow finding build credibility over time more reliably than infrequent long-form pieces.[^37]

### 4.4 Conference Abstracts

The abstract's job is to get a sceptical reviewer to believe that this talk will teach an audience something specific that they do not already know.[^51][^52][^53]

**Credibility moves:**

- **Name your actual finding in the abstract**, not *"the results will be discussed"* — if you cannot state the result, you are not ready to submit[^51]
- **Use the story arc**: opening (why this matters, specifically, not in general), challenge (exact problem you faced), action (what you did, with named methods), resolution (what you found)[^54]
- **One concrete number or named finding** does more credibility work than a paragraph of general claims
- **Avoid the motivation sentence that applies to any talk in the field**: *"As machine learning continues to reshape industry…"* — replace with *"We discovered that 30% of our false positives were caused by a single date-handling edge case"*
- **No filler phrases**: *"it can be argued that," "it is commonly acknowledged that," "I wish to propose the argument that"* — these are hedging tax on your abstract's word budget[^52]

### 4.5 Founder / About Copy

The about page is where authority-cosplay concentrates most densely because it is explicitly about self-presentation. The genre defaults produce exactly the AI register described in Section 1.

**Credibility moves:**

- **Lead with what you have built, not who you are**: *"I built the reconciliation system at [Company] and then rebuilt it when it failed at 10× scale"* > *"I am a passionate technologist committed to excellence"*
- **Name the specific domain and scope**: vague claims of expertise (*"cloud, data, and digital transformation"*) are lower-credibility than narrow ones (*"PostgreSQL query optimisation at fintech scale"*)[^21]
- **One honest failure or limitation** is worth three accomplishments: it signals that the rest of the page is also honest
- **Use the second-person sparingly or not at all**: about copy that addresses the reader (*"Are you struggling with X? I can help"*) performs a commercial register that undercuts credibility in practitioner contexts
- **Current tense for what is currently true**: not *"I am deeply passionate about…"* but *"I am currently building…"* or just *"I build…"*

### 4.6 Presenting Results and Metrics Credibly

A persistent slop pattern is **metric inflation without context**: reporting a number without the denominator, baseline, or conditions. This reads as worse than no number at all.

**Rules for credible metrics:**

1. **Always state the baseline**: *"from X to Y"* not just *"Y"*
2. **State the conditions**: time period, environment, sample size, what was held constant
3. **Name the limit of your measurement**: *"we tracked this for three months post-launch; we do not have longer-term data"*
4. **Do not round dramatically**: *"47%"* is more credible than *"nearly half"* is more credible than *"significant uplift"*
5. **Distinguish your result from your model's prediction**: what you projected vs. what actually happened
6. **Acknowledge confounders**: what else changed at the same time that might have contributed

The "proof economy" framing captures the 2026 reality: audiences expect receipts. A case study with three specific, bounded metrics outperforms a paragraph of qualitative success language.[^4]

## 5. Quick Reference

### 5.1 The Kill-List: Banned Words and Patterns

**Lexical bans (in professional practitioner contexts):**

| Category | Ban these |
|---|---|
| Landscape clichés | *ever-evolving landscape, rapidly shifting landscape, in today's fast-paced world, in the digital age, in an era characterised by* |
| Motion-nowhere verbs | *leverage, harness, unlock, elevate, empower, foster, navigate, propel, pave the way, shed light on, resonate with, supercharge* |
| Empty adjectives | *pivotal, crucial, transformative, innovative, cutting-edge, disruptive, holistic, seamless, groundbreaking, game-changing* |
| Filler nouns | *landscape, ecosystem, tapestry, realm, paradigm, synergy, stakeholders (as a general noun), journey (metaphorical)* |
| Ornate substitutes | *delve, myriad, testament to, cornerstone of, treasure trove, intricate interplay* |
| Credential-puffery | *seasoned, passionate (in bio copy), results-driven, thought leader, visionary, guru* |

**Structural bans:**

| Pattern | Why |
|---|---|
| *In today's… / In an era…* openers | Pure AI tell; commits to nothing |
| *Not X, but Y* negative parallelisms | AI manufactured-contrarianism structure |
| *Rule-of-three adjective stacks* | Signals padding over thought |
| *False ranges* ("from X to Y") | Implies spectrum, specifies nothing |
| *In conclusion / To summarise / Overall* | Restates what was just said |
| *It is important to note that…* | Hedge-tax on a sentence that should just state its claim |
| *More than ever before / unprecedented* | Unverifiable intensity claims |
| *Research shows / Studies suggest* (without citation) | Phantom evidence |
| *Some experts argue / Critics say* (without names) | Phantom authority |
| Formulaic "Challenges" and "Future Prospects" section headers | Rigid AI essay scaffolding |

### 5.2 The Positive Checklist: Credibility Moves

Use this as a pre-publication check. A credible practitioner piece should satisfy most of these.

**On specificity:**
- [ ] At least one specific number with its conditions and baseline
- [ ] At least one named tool, method, version, or environment
- [ ] Claims bounded to the specific context in which they were observed

**On showing the work:**
- [ ] At least one thing that did not work, with a reason
- [ ] At least one tradeoff named explicitly (what this approach costs you)
- [ ] At least one place where the advice does not apply or breaks down

**On earned opinion:**
- [ ] Opinions grounded in first-person observation, not in general authority
- [ ] Disagreements or alternatives acknowledged where they exist
- [ ] Confidence calibrated to what was actually tested vs. assumed

**On structure:**
- [ ] Opens on substance, not context-setting
- [ ] No compulsive summary paragraph unless the piece is genuinely long enough to warrant one
- [ ] Sentence rhythm varies — mix of short declaratives and longer qualified sentences
- [ ] No listicle conversion of reasoning that should remain as argument

**On tone:**
- [ ] Hedges selectively — only where genuine uncertainty exists
- [ ] Boosts when warranted — uses *clearly, definitely, reliably* where things are, in fact, clear, definite, and reliable
- [ ] No credential-prefacing (*"As someone who has worked in X for Y years…"*) — let the content carry the authority
- [ ] No performed humility that contradicts the confidence of the advice

**On format-specific rules:**
- [ ] Case studies: opens with a number; includes at least one failed approach
- [ ] Portfolio writeups: states the specific decision made and why, not just the outcome
- [ ] Technical posts: names the exact environment and scale; acknowledges where advice does not transfer
- [ ] Abstracts: states the actual finding, not a promise to discuss it
- [ ] Bio copy: uses active verbs describing things built, not adjectives describing qualities possessed

- **[Strong]**: Multiple independent primary or peer-reviewed sources; consistent findings across sources; current (2024–2026)
- **[Moderate]**: Single strong primary source or multiple practitioner sources; plausible but not cross-validated in peer-reviewed literature; or advice that is well-established but from pre-2024 sources
